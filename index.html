Once upon a time there was a little boy named Benjamin. He lived with his mommy and daddy at 190 S. Oxford Street.

None of the neighbors or his friends at school knew it, but Benjamin was a powerful sorcerer. He came from a family of sorcerers, but had exceptional talent. His mommy and daddy were teaching him and he was learning fast.

Already, he had learned shape-shifting, levitation (both of himself and objects), unlocking spells, light spells, spells of darkness, object summoning, antidote charms for poisons of all kinds, and he could easily read the seven magical languages, so could access all the spellbooks in the house on his own.

Occasionally he got into trouble, because not all magic is created the same and some of it refuses to be totally controlled even by powerful wizards. Indeed, the most powerful magic has a mind of its own and must be tamed and coaxed and coerced, each spell on its own terms. It requires improvisation, often empathy and above all, patience, which is one of the most difficult aspects of the magical disciplines for any young sorcerer to master.

At any rate, one day, Benjamin was awoken from a deep sleep by a tremendous clattering at his window. The clock on his bureau said “3:05am.” Who on earth would be trying to rouse him at this hour? And then a chill ran through him: how great an emergency must it be if it could not wait until morning.

He opened the blinds and there saw a strange creature indeed. It resembled a rather large pink octopus, but with the piercing eyes of an owl. And on the end of each of its many legs it had a birdlike talon, which were the source of the noise. Also, unlike an octopus, it was decidedly out of the water. Benjamin wrenched the window open and the creature – larger than Benjamin had thought; bigger than a cat – flopped into the room and on to the floor with a great exhalation. Presumably, Benjamin thought, an exhausted sigh.

“Water,” it croaked in a peculiar accent. Benjamin ran to the kitchen, fetched a large bowl of water, and the creature immediately slithered into it, face down. Its whole body pulsed orange as it drank.

“Ahhh,” it said. “Thank you.”

“I’m Benjamin,” said Benjamin. “Who are you and how can I help?”

“I am…” the creature began and then made an odd, slightly disgusting sound in its throat that must have been its name. “But you can call me Egbert.”

“Hello, Egbert,” said Benjamin.”

“Now then,” said the creature, a little fussily. “Without further ado: the problem. I belong to a race as yet undiscovered by humankind, which is very much how we prefer it. But what this means is that every so often, without meaning to do harm, humankind will do something that changes our environment. Once they tried to build an oil rig that heated the water and threatened our food supply.”

“What did you do,” asked Benjamin.

“Why, we sabotaged it until they gave up and tried somewhere else. Another time a diving company started exploring right around our city. That was alarming,” said the creature, shuddering at the memory. “In that case we had to be a bit more aggressive. We cut the tubes to their breathing tanks, sending them back to the surface over and over until they, too, gave up. But now…” Here the creature slumped. Benjamin knew he was about to hear the real problem.

“Now, they have built a drill. Practically overnight. It’s already pumping away.”

“What danger does it pose?” Benjamin asked.

“Like the oil rig, we are certain it will eventually malfunction and fill our city with oil and that would mean the end… of our civilization,” Egbert said with a sniffle.

“Why don’t you sabotage it like the oil rig?”

“Well, we’ve tried, of course, but it’s not easy. The humans operate it remotely, so we can’t scare them the same way. And it’s like an armored monster – we’ve looked for ways in, weaknesses, but so far have found none. This is why I have made the journey from the sea overland to reach you. Can you help? Oh, please say ‘yes.’”

Benjamin scratched his chin. “Well, I’m sure I can help. The question is only how. Let me talk to my parents.”

“Quickly, please. At any moment, the armored monster could begin belching death and destruction upon my people.”

Benjamin smiled. “Don’t worry. This’ll be quick.”

Before Egbert quite knew what had happened, he was sitting happily in a miniature kiddie pool in the trunk of Benjamin’s family’s car, his mommy driving and his daddy in the back seat discussing magical strategy with Benjamin.

“What we want,” Benjamin said, “is to do something that doesn’t invite investigation. We don’t want more humans around than there already are.”

“Sure,” said Benjamin’s daddy, “but what could that be? We blow it up, they come looking for the pieces. We freeze it, they try to repair it.”

“What if we just… disappear it?” said Benjamin.

“By jove,” said Benjamin’s daddy. “I believe you’ve got it. But that’s not a one-sorcerer job.”

“No,” said Benjamin. “The three of us will have to work together.”

Benjamin’s daddy held up one hand in a kind of karate chop and they did their secret handshake.
